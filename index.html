Body condition guide

Calories are only half the story — your dog's body tells you whether the amount is right. Vets score body condition from 1 (far too thin) to 9 (obese). Check with your hands and eyes once a month:

Score 1–3 · Underweight

Too thin

Ribs, spine and hip bones are easy to see, not just feel. The waist is exaggerated and the belly tucks up sharply. Increase food by about 10% and see your vet if weight keeps dropping — parasites, dental pain and illness can all hide behind a thin dog.

Score 4–5 · Ideal

Just right

Ribs are easy to feel under a thin fat layer but not visible. From above there is a clear waist behind the ribs; from the side the belly tucks up. This is the target — hold calories steady and re-check monthly.

Score 6–9 · Overweight

Too heavy

You have to press to find the ribs, the waist is faint or gone, and the belly may sag. Cut calories by about 10%, count treats honestly, and re-weigh every two weeks. Extra weight shortens a dog's life and strains joints and the heart.

How the RER / DER method works

RER (Resting Energy Requirement) is the energy a dog burns just staying alive at rest. Because metabolism does not scale in a straight line with size, vets calculate it as 70 × (weight in kg)0.75. A 30 lb (13.6 kg) dog has an RER of about 494 kcal per day.

DER (Daily Energy Requirement) multiplies RER by a factor that reflects real life: puppies building a body need up to 3× RER, a typical neutered adult about 1.6×, an intact adult 1.8×, and a dog that needs to lose weight is usually fed at about 1.0× the RER of its target weight. This calculator combines the standard life-stage factors with small adjustments for activity and body condition, which is why two dogs of the same weight can honestly need quite different amounts of food.

Every dog is an individual, so treat the result as a well-informed starting point — the scale and the body-condition check over the next month tell you the truth.

Measuring tip: weigh food with a kitchen scale for the first week. A "cup" scooped generously can hold 20–30% more kibble than a level cup — the most common reason dogs gain weight on the "right" amount of food.

Why the bag's feeding guide is only a starting point

Feeding guides printed on dog food bags are written for an average, intact, fairly active dog — and they tend to be generous, because a slightly overfed dog looks "happy" at the bowl. If your dog is neutered, older, or a professional napper, the bag guide can overshoot real needs by 20% or more. Starting from calories instead of cups fixes that: calories are the one number every food shares, whatever the brand.

Remember the 10% rule while you portion: treats, chews and table scraps should make up no more than about 10% of daily calories. A single pig ear can hold 180 kcal — a fifth of a medium dog's whole day.

Toxic Food Checker for Dogs

Search a human food to see whether it is safe, needs caution, or is toxic for dogs. When a food is marked toxic, even a small amount can matter — call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435, a fee may apply) if your dog has eaten it.

If your dog just ate something toxic: do not wait for symptoms and do not make your dog vomit unless a veterinarian tells you to. Note what was eaten, roughly how much, and your dog's weight, then call your vet or an animal poison hotline immediately — minutes matter with xylitol and grapes in particular.

About

About DogDietCare

DogDietCare is a free, independent resource for dog owners in the United States who want a straight answer to a deceptively simple question: how much should I actually feed my dog?

Most feeding advice online is either a vague bag-back chart or a sales pitch. We built DogDietCare around the same tools veterinary professionals use — the RER/DER calorie method, body condition scoring, and established food-safety lists — and turned them into calculators and checkers anyone can use in under a minute.

What you will find here

  • Working tools, not just articles — a calorie calculator, a feeding amount and cost calculator, and a toxic food checker.
  • Charts that respect real life — puppy feeding schedules and size-by-size adult feeding guides, labelled as starting points to adjust from.
  • Original guides — every article on this site is written for DogDietCare, in plain English, with no copied content.

Our limits, stated plainly

We are not veterinarians and this site is not a clinic. Everything here is educational. Dogs with kidney disease, pancreatitis, diabetes, allergies or other conditions need diets designed with their vet, and no calculator can replace that. Please read our Disclaimer before acting on any number this site gives you.
